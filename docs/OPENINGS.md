# The first three seconds: nine ways to open a short clip

A planning sheet for the opening of a short video: what happens in the first three seconds, what to write in the video prompt, and how much evidence stands behind each approach. It comes from the "hook" library of the studio this one grew from (2026-10-17), rewritten here. No opening below has been rendered or measured on this studio.

## Read this first

- **Nothing here predicts reach.** The research behind this sheet found popularity mostly unpredictable: social influence makes outcomes unequal and hard to call (Salganik, Dodds and Watts 2006, a music-market experiment with 14,341 people). Use these as craft choices, never as a forecast.
- **Almost all the evidence is about sound and music.** Applying it to pictures is inference, and each row says so.
- Grades: **B** means a single study or a large data analysis, **C** means industry practice or a trend, **D** means our own inference. No opening here reaches **A** (replicated, or published by a platform).
- The sound notes are plans for an audio track added after the render. The picture prompt describes only what is seen. For text on screen, add it after the render. Don't ask a video model to draw words.

## The nine openings

### 1. Surprise break (B for music, D for pictures)
A familiar frame, then one controlled break.
- **0 to 1.2 s:** a calm situation people recognise, so the viewer can guess what comes next.
- **1.2 to 2.2 s:** one clear break of that guess: something arrives, falls, changes size or answers back.
- **2.2 to 3 s:** the result stays on screen long enough to read.
- **Write:** a steady, ordinary opening frame. Exactly one break, as a physical event with a start, a movement and an end. Harmless or funny, never distressing. One camera decision and no cut.
- **Sound:** silence or a quiet bed, then a single hit on the break.
- **Why:** music research supports "a familiar frame with one controlled surprise" (Gold et al. 2019; Cheung et al. 2019). The strongest of the nine for music; for pictures it is our inference.

### 2. Sound first: a sting or a riser into the cut (B for the mechanism)
- **0 to 0.8 s:** a short rising sound, or silence, over a plain frame.
- **0.8 to 1.2 s:** the hit lands exactly on the first visual event.
- **1.2 to 3 s:** the picture pays the hit off and leaves room after it.
- **Write:** one visual event near 1 s for the hit to land on, and a simple frame before it so the hit has contrast.
- **Avoid:** stacked hits, a hit on every cut, or dialogue over the hit.
- **Why:** startle depends on a fast onset, and a rising level makes the hit land harder (Blumenthal and Berg 1986; Olsen and Stevens 2010). These are lab results on sounds; nothing ties them to a video opening.

### 3. Instant motion in frame one (C)
- **0 to 0.5 s:** the subject is already mid-action, with no build-up and no fade-in.
- **0.5 to 3 s:** the action develops and reaches a clear point.
- **Write:** start in the middle of an action. Put subject motion and camera motion in separate sentences, and keep one main action.
- **Why:** a quarter of streamed songs are skipped within 5 s (Montecchio, Roy and Pachet 2020), and advertising guidance says to make the point early. "Motion in frame one" is a rule of thumb with no published number for video.

### 4. Payoff first (C)
- **0 to 1.5 s:** the best moment or the finished result, in full.
- **1.5 to 3 s:** a beat that rewinds or asks how it happened.
- **Write:** the end result, finished and clear, described in concrete detail. Then a beat that points back.
- **Why:** pop songs have moved their hooks forward (Léveillé Gauvin 2018). That is a trend in songs, not a finding about video.

### 5. An everyday moment, seen first-hand (C)
- **0 to 1 s:** a first-person or phone-held view of an ordinary moment.
- **1 to 2 s:** the small thing everyone recognises happens.
- **2 to 3 s:** a reaction beat.
- **Write:** ordinary light, one small, recognisable event, a believable imperfection. No spectacle, and never a real person's name or likeness.

### 6. A loop that ends where it starts (C, the loop effect itself untested)
- **Write:** a motion that returns to its own start (a swing, a spin, a pour, a turn and back). Describe the first and last state in the same words, and keep the camera fixed so the frames match. On MiniMax H3 the same still as first and last frame makes this a first-and-last-frame job.
- **Sound:** a bed that sits at the same level at both ends.
- **Avoid:** a camera move that doesn't return, an ending that resolves the story, or a fade-out.

### 7. A text line (C)
- **0 to 0.5 s:** a short line of text is already readable. It is added after the render.
- **Write:** a clean picture with a calm area left for the text. One line of at most 8 words making one promise, which the picture keeps.

### 8. Visual contrast (D)
- **0 to 1 s:** hard light, one saturated colour or an unusual scale. **1 to 2 s:** one change, such as a light switching or the framing jumping closer. **2 to 3 s:** the subject is clear.
- **Avoid:** a muddy, low-contrast opening, or a title card or logo first.
- **Why:** folk practice. Nothing in the research measured it for video.

### 9. A question left open (D)
- **0 to 1 s:** a puzzling, partial or hidden image, or a short question on screen. **1 to 2 s:** a clue, not the answer. **2 to 3 s:** the viewer is pointed forward.
- **Avoid:** answering the question in the opening, or a question the picture already answers.
- **Why:** included because it is common. We hold no evidence for it.

## For all nine

One camera decision in the opening, at most one cut, and nothing distressing. Short sounds, sound mixing and loudness have their own research notes in the crew's audio and music guides (`studio/crew_notes/model_families/03-Audio-FX-separation-foley-models/dossier/GUIDE.md`, `studio/crew_notes/model_families/17-Music-song-models/dossier/GUIDE.md`).
